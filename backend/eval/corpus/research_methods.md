# Research Methods — Chapter Excerpt

## Literature Review

A literature review synthesises existing work on a topic. The four standard
movements are: (1) **scope** — define the boundary of what you'll cover,
(2) **search** — locate relevant sources through databases such as Google
Scholar, Scopus, or domain-specific indexes, (3) **appraise** — evaluate
each source's methodology and relevance, and (4) **synthesise** — organise
the findings into a narrative, not a list of summaries.

A systematic review goes further: it pre-registers a protocol, uses
explicit inclusion/exclusion criteria, and often reports inter-rater
reliability for study selection. PRISMA is the dominant reporting
standard in medicine and adjacent fields.

## Qualitative vs Quantitative

Quantitative research tests hypotheses with numerical data and statistical
inference; the emphasis is on replicability and generalisation.
Qualitative research produces thick descriptions of phenomena through
interviews, ethnography, or textual analysis; it prioritises depth and
context over generalisation.

Mixed-methods designs combine the two — for example, a quantitative survey
whose surprising results are interpreted via follow-up interviews. Pragmatic
researchers argue the choice should be driven by the question, not by
methodological allegiance.

## Citation Styles

APA (author–date) is dominant in the social sciences and uses in-text
citations like (Smith, 2021). IEEE uses numbered brackets in the order of
appearance. Chicago offers both a notes-and-bibliography style for the
humanities and an author–date style similar to APA.

Regardless of style, every paraphrase must be cited; copying three or more
consecutive words without attribution counts as plagiarism at most
universities.
